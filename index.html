How to read the data: Life expectancy here means the expected age at death for a person who has reached age 40, not life expectancy at birth. The pooled estimates and individual-year estimates come from separate source tables. Income percentile does not necessarily represent the same dollar amount in every year.

About this project

Dataset & question

We explore the relationship between household income percentile and life expectancy among adults in the United States using data from the study The Association Between Income and Life Expectancy in the United States, 2001–2014. Table 1 provides national estimates pooled across years; Table 2 provides separate estimates for each year, gender, and income percentile. Our focus is whether the income-related life expectancy gap differs by gender and changes over time.

Visual design & interactions

We use a line chart for the income gradient because income percentile has a natural ordering. A second line chart makes changes over time easy to compare. The same two selected income percentiles appear throughout the dashboard, with matching colors for their annual trends. We considered bar charts, but lines better communicate ordered relationships and changes over time.

  • Dynamic filtering (Category I): Change gender, year, life expectancy measure, and income percentiles using dropdowns.
  • Linked selection (Category I): Clicking the income curve changes a comparison percentile and updates the trends; clicking a trend year updates the income chart.
  • Details on demand (Category II): Hover over either chart to inspect exact values.
  • Highlighting (Category II): Selected percentiles and the active year are emphasized with colored markers and guide lines.

Interpretation & limitations

The charts describe associations, not evidence that income alone causes a change in lifespan. These are estimates for people who have reached age 40, and annual estimates may fluctuate. The underlying observations cover 2001–2014, so the results should not be treated as current-day life expectancy figures. The race-adjusted and unadjusted measures are distinct estimates; the selector lets readers examine both without mixing them.
